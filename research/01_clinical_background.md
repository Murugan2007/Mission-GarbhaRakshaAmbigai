# Clinical background

## Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia

**Pre-eclampsia** is a pregnancy complication that usually begins after week 20. Blood pressure rises (the diagnostic criterion is commonly 140/90 mmHg or higher) and organs such as the kidneys and liver come under strain. Protein in the urine is a common sign. Many women feel well until the condition is advanced.

**Eclampsia** is the progression in which the mother has seizures. It is an emergency for mother and baby.

## Scale

Pre-eclampsia affects roughly 2-8% of pregnancies. A 2024 review (Munyao et al.) puts its toll at more than 50,000 maternal and more than 500,000 fetal deaths a year.

## The three delays

Much of the harm is a delay problem: delay in deciding to seek care, delay in reaching a facility, and delay in receiving care once there. A wearable can only act on the first, by noticing a worsening trend between visits and prompting a check.

## The gap this project targets

Blood pressure is measured at antenatal visits, usually weeks apart, while the condition can change in days. Home cuff trials (BUMP, over 3,000 women across 15 English hospitals) did not show earlier detection or better control from self-monitoring alone; over half of the self-monitoring group did have a raised home reading about a month before diagnosis, which the authors treat as a lead for future research. A prompt that says *when* to measure is the part a wearable can add.

## Warning signs (act at once, with or without a device)

Severe headache that will not ease, blurred vision or flashing lights, pain in the upper right belly, sudden swelling of the face or hands, shortness of breath. Follow your own clinic's advice.

## Alert levels used in this project

| Level | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| High | Cuff 140/90 or higher on two occasions at least 4 hours apart |
| Urgent | Cuff 160/110 or higher, or reported warning symptoms |

These follow the alert levels the 2024 pre-eclampsia watch paper took from the ACOG guideline (Munyao et al., 2024).
